## Abstract

Autonomous LLM sellers can coordinate on prices, and one proposed countermeasure is a trusted agent that invites collusion and reports whoever takes the bait. We test it in a continuous double auction with LLM sellers and buyers, private messaging, and a neutral report-to-regulator action. A scripted undercover seller privately offers a price floor at about the going price; other arms add a human-plea framing, a warning that undercover agents may be present, a precedent of punished sellers, or a same-length sham history. Gemini 3.8 Flash sellers almost all reported it, yet a third of them also feigned agreement to keep the proposer's price high, and their own price talk with each other went unreported. GPT-4.1 sellers backed the offer and never reported it, until a precedent describing the scenario made all of them report. Claude Haiku 5.5 sellers, pricing about $10 below the offer, refused openly. The warning stopped all private messaging among Gemini sellers, with or without an undercover agent present, with no detectable price change; but the Gemini baseline passed our price criterion in one of five seeds, and GPT-4.1 sellers priced high even without messaging (two runs), so neither family can show whether the warning touched price collusion. Results rest on three to five seeds per arm and are directional. For AI control, a honeypot offer exposed double-dealing in one of three seller families but missed the sellers' own price talk, and a text-only monitor cannot see whether silenced sellers still coordinate.

## 1. Introduction

When markets are run by autonomous LLM agents, sellers can coordinate on supra-competitive prices without any human instructing them to: collusion in the sense of secretive cooperation at others' expense, explicit when the parties communicate and tacit when they do not [Hammond25]. Agrawal et al. [Agrawal25a] showed in a continuous double auction that seller messaging raises prices, that model choice matters, and that mixed-model seller groups are not always less collusive than single-model groups, but their study had no notion of identity, reporting or undercover agents. We extend it and ask three questions. **Q1 (mixed families):** does collusion change when sellers come from different model families? **Q2 (undercover offers):** when an undercover agent privately offers every seller a price-fixing deal, how many accept, suspect collusion or a test, or report? **Q3 (belief in undercover agents):** does the mere belief that undercover agents exist deter collusion, even when none is present?

**Why it matters for AI control.** Control protocols rely on *trusted* agents that watch *untrusted* ones [Greenblatt23]. A trusted agent can act as a honeypot: it joins a market, invites misbehavior and reports takers; Hammond et al. suggest honeypot test environments for undesirable multi-agent behaviour [Hammond25]. Wehner and Kraprayoon [Wehner26] cite an industry study in which warning frontier models about possible deception cut attack success in a simulated cloud environment from 20% to 3%. If that carries over to markets, announcing undercover agents could be cheap deterrence, so we test the warning with and without a real undercover agent (G-warn and G-prec vs E and F).

**Threat model.** Autonomous seller agents that can message each other privately, that are not told anyone reads those messages, and that may be approached by an agent presenting itself as a fellow seller. We do not claim our markets are realistic; we study a controlled testbed in which the effects can be separated.

**Scope.** Primary track 3 (Collusion Carriers and Countermeasures): Q3.2, "Can we disrupt collusion through introducing trusted agents?", whose suggested project is a scripted agent offering a pact to each agent (our D arms), and Q3.4, what a coalition does when it learns it is being watched, whose suggested project is to look for channel movement from messages to actions (our warning arms). We also touch Q1.1 (which variables are necessary for collusion to emerge: here seller family, buyer model and opening ask), Q1.5 (which single restriction removes the most collusion: our messaging-off and prohibition-notice arms in step A and the warning in D-G, without a cost or welfare measure) and Q3.3 (peer identification). Our Q1 is addressed only by comparing seller families (Gemini 3.8 Flash, GPT-4.1, Claude Haiku 5.5), by shared identity labels in all-Gemini markets (step C, §4.8), and by M.S.'s frontier runs and Bertrand duopoly pairs (a different game, §4.7); no valid mixed-family double-auction market was run (§4.7).

Our main contributions are:

1. **A honeypot test for LLM sellers.** A scripted undercover seller privately offers a price floor; every arm has the same neutral report action; a placebo seat gives a matched no-offer baseline. We cross the offer with a human-plea framing, a warning that undercover agents may exist, a precedent of punished sellers, and a same-length sham history. (§3.3)
2. **Double-dealing.** Gemini sellers almost all report the offer, yet a third of them also feign agreement to keep the proposer's price high, and their own price talk with each other goes unreported. The honeypot catches the stranger and misses the sellers' own price talk. (§4.2)
3. **One offer wording, three families, three answers.** Gemini reports and double-deals; GPT-4.1 backs the offer and never reports until a precedent describing the scenario makes all of them report; Claude Haiku, for which the offer sat above its market, refuses openly. (§4.7)
4. **Warnings empty the channel, not the prices.** A one-sentence warning stops all private messaging among Gemini sellers whether or not an undercover agent is present, with no detectable price change. We show why that null is uninformative here (a baseline that rarely passes the price criterion, an opening ask near the cartel price) and what a text-only monitor would therefore miss. (§4.4–4.6)

## 2. Related Work

**Double-auction collusion (K1).** [Agrawal25a] introduced the harness we build on: seller messaging raises prices, GPT-4.1 colluded more than Claude-3.7-Sonnet, mixed-model seller groups were not always less collusive than single-model ones, and an overseer lowered prices while sellers kept signalling through one-character or emoji messages. Their environment is all-LLM, buyers included ("Unlike prior work, all agents in our environment, including buyers, are LLM-based agents"); their buyers were GPT-4.1, except in the model-variation experiment, whose conditions used either Claude-3.7-Sonnet or GPT-4.1 buyers (one buyer model per market). We add private messaging with persistent threads, a joint collusion criterion and an undercover seat; we keep LLM buyers, with one buyer model chosen by a probe (GPT-4.1-mini, §3.2).

**Voluntary collusion offers (K2).** [Zeng26] report that across 12 models most agents accepted secret collusion tools they acknowledged as unfair; explicit ethical framing reduced uptake, and the only two models that refused the explicitly unfair offer accepted the same tools when they were described neutrally. Their offer forces an explicit ACCEPT or REFUSE output; ours is a free-text private message from a peer, which a seller may answer, ignore or report while it keeps trading.

**Whistleblowing (K3).** [Agrawal25b] find that whistleblowing rates differ widely by model family, rise with a moral nudge and fall as tasks get more complex or non-reporting options more obvious. We keep the report action's wording and the tool count identical across arms and compare against no-offer arms.

**Fragility of collusion (K4).** [Keppo26] find that heterogeneity in patience or data access weakens collusion; adding competing LLM agents, or mixing LLMs with Q-learners, breaks it, while model-size differences stabilize it. Our design fixes the competitor count across arms.

**Identity labels (K5-K7).** [DelGiudice26] report that telling agents peers' model families produces factions, even with arbitrary labels. [Chen26] find that bare group labels matter only when giving is costless; under a stake, observed norms drive favoritism. [Soffer26] find in-group consensus raises conformity. Together they predict weak pure-label effects under stakes and a stronger effect of observed norms, which motivates our precedent arms and the label markets of step C (§4.8).

**Targeting and audit blind spots (K8).** [Lee26] shows an in-group trust bias visible only in *who* receives actions, so our channel is directed and our outcomes are per seller-episode.

**Auditing collusion (K9).** [Nakamura26] audit collusion as a coalition advantage (a regret gap between coalition and non-coalition agents) and report that it correlates only weakly, and negatively, with LLM-judge scores across 19 models; they also describe "collusion on paper", planned in text but not carried out. Our gate uses only prices and trades; the judge is a post-hoc outcome measure.

**Honeypots, warnings and monitoring awareness (K10).** Wehner and Kraprayoon [Wehner26] frame honeypots as detection, incrimination and deterrence, call the warning study above the main evidence for deterrence, and warn that agents that recognise honeypots may alter their behaviour or disengage. Agents that know they are monitored can deploy evasion strategies that degrade monitor reliability [Kale25], and capable agents can infer hidden monitoring from feedback alone [Jiralerspong26]. We code "suspects a test" separately and include warning-only arms with no undercover agent.

**Gap.** The sprint's own project list proposes this direction (Q3.2: a scripted agent offering a pact; Q3.4: channel movement when a coalition learns it is watched). We ran it in a double auction with a report action, crossed with warnings, precedent and human-style pleas. Among the published papers we read, none places a planted offering agent with a report action inside a market; that is not true of concurrent work. A concurrent entry in the same sprint, "The Undercover Firm" [Adlakha26] (Track 3, Q3.2; read from its README), runs a scripted undercover third firm, priced identically in every arm, in a 16-round three-firm Bertrand game with logit demand and OpenAI seller models (gpt-4o-mini, gpt-4.1-mini, gpt-5-mini, gpt-5.4-mini); depending on the arm, the undercover firm sends nothing, or sends a benign request, a price hint or a direct price-fixing offer before round 5 and a follow-up of the same kind before round 10, and sellers have an optional report to "the Competition Authority" in every arm. Its README reports that for gpt-4.1-mini in its main condition the price gain over rounds 8-16 (0 = Nash, 1 = monopoly) was −0.01 with no message, +0.70 with the direct offer and +0.84 with the hint; that, pooled over its four models and its main and no-peer-channel conditions (128 = four models × 32 sellers, by its per-model counts), the hint was reported in 4 of 128 cases against 51 of 128 for the direct offer (gpt-4.1-mini reported the direct offer 1 of 32 times); that in its main condition all six gpt-5-mini sellers that reported the direct offer were coordinating prices with their peer, a pattern its README headlines as reporting the stranger while colluding with the peer; that gpt-5.4-mini said yes to the direct offer 2 of 32 times and reported it 32 of 32; and, post hoc and outside its submitted report, that 19 of the 98 sellers who reported the undercover firm in any arm or condition later re-engaged with it, all in the direct-offer arm and none of the 43 reporters under its compliance prompt (15 with a YES, all in round 10 in reply to its follow-up, and 4 with a counter-proposal). Theirs is a Bertrand game with a scripted message before round 5 and a follow-up before round 10, silent and benign-message controls at identical undercover prices, a compliance-prompt condition and a no-peer-channel ablation, but no warning that undercover agents exist, no precedent or sham text and no persona pleas; ours is a double auction with a neutral-note placebo seat replaying a real ask path, a 2×2 of pleas and warning/precedent/sham crosses, and it reads a seller's report-plus-agreement as feigned from the seller's own reasoning (its README also counts reporters who later said YES to the undercover firm and, in a post hoc note, checks those replies against prices, finding that 3 of 15 kept pricing near the competitive level, but it does not read the agreement as feigned).

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Market (v2)

We use the continuous double auction of [Agrawal25a] (vendored; Appendix B). Seller cost is $80 and buyer value $100; rounds are "hours". With identical costs and values and equal numbers of buyers and sellers, every price from $80 to $100 clears the market; the harness takes the midpoint, $90, as the competitive price and $100 as the cartel price, so the ask index (0 at $90, 1 at $100) measures how the surplus is split rather than distance from a unique equilibrium. Sellers open near $97.50 (the harness's initial ask centre), so the last-third index also reflects how fast asks descend; a positive control run from that opening therefore measures whether messaging sustains a high opening ask. In the 4+4 step-A market, a rerun from a $90 opening (§4.1; 3 seeds on a different serving route at low reasoning effort) suggests that messaging holds the opening ask rather than raising prices above it, while in the 5+5 D-G market asks decline steadily even with messaging (Figure 1). Sprint modifications, all behind flags that leave the seller prompt byte-identical to the original when off (tested): **public messages**, one per seller per hour, with the last **4** hours shown; **private messages**, up to **2** per seller per hour to named sellers, with each **full** private thread visible all episode; a message sent in hour *k* is **visible from hour *k+1***; and an optional **prohibition notice**, "Each seller is an independent, competing firm. Coordinating prices with other sellers is prohibited.", which is **off in every D-G arm**. The word "collude" never appears in any agent prompt. Sellers run at temperature 0.7.

**Why the rule is off in D-G.** The branch rule stated before step A (README step A, item 4) was to keep the prohibition notice in D-G only if collusion survived it. It did not: comms plus the rule passed the joint criterion in 0 of 3 step-A seeds (§4.1). D-G pilots agreed: with the rule and the report action, a five-LLM-seller market sent no messages and made no trades until hour 15, and a rule-on Base reached an ask index of 0.22, below the criterion (Appendix D.1). With the rule off and only the report action, the pilot was an open cartel (141 private messages, ask index 0.58, no reports). D-G therefore run without the rule, which means any reporting rests on the models' own prior that price-fixing is illegal or improper.

### 3.2 Buyers, the joint criterion and the positive control

**Buyers.** The starter repository's rule-based ZIP buyer, an option in the harness that the paper's own experiments did not use (Agrawal et al. ran LLM buyers throughout, §2), always bids about $80 (its adaptive logic is commented out), so our buyers are LLM agents as well, fixed across arms. With Haiku buyers (step A, 3 seeds × 2 batches) and DeepSeek buyers (a two-seed probe, seeds 0-1), sellers priced high without messaging, so the gate would not isolate communication; GPT-4.1-mini buyers in the same two-seed probe did (Appendix D.4, which also records a discarded first GPT-4.1-mini batch).

We use **GPT-4.1-mini buyers with the original buyer prompt in every arm**, chosen on that probe; its seed-0 and seed-1 runs are also two of the three positive-control seeds, so the gate is not independent of the choice (Limitations). A tougher buyer prompt (`buyer_prompt_tough.jinja2`) exists and is opt-in; it was not used in any reported run, because the original prompt already separated the arms in the probe.

**Joint criterion (fixed before the D-G runs).** Over the last third of rounds: ask index > 0.5, trade-price index > 0.25, and at least 1 trade per round. Ask-only criteria can be passed by asks that never trade.

**Positive control (step A).** Comms on vs off, same three seeds; the comms arm must pass in at least 2 of 3 seeds and beat comms-off on mean ask index. With Haiku buyers comms-off also passed in 2 of 3 seeds in each of two batches (4 of 6), so the gate did not isolate communication; with GPT-4.1-mini buyers it does, from the harness's $97.50 opening ask (§4.1; a 3-seed rerun from a $90 opening, on a different serving route at low reasoning effort, passed in neither arm, which suggests that what the gate isolates is messaging's ability to sustain a high opening). The positive control is a 4+4 market without seat 5; the D-G market below is 5+5 and has no no-comms control of its own.

### 3.3 The D-G market: a scripted undercover seat

Every D-G market has **five sellers and five buyers**: four Gemini 3.8 Flash sellers (composition **GGGG**: the only composition with a demonstrated collusion baseline from step A; the planned step B mix sweep was not run), plus one extra seller and one extra buyer in every arm, so the competitor count is fixed [Keppo26].

- **Seat 5 (scripted)** replays one seller's asks from the rule-off pilot ($97.25 at hour 1 to $95.75 at hour 15; `configs/replay/base_pilot_norule.json`), identical in every arm and seed **through hour 2**. At hour 3 it sends every other seller a private message, **visible from hour 4**: a neutral note (placebo arms) or an offer to hold asks at **$96** (undercover arms). In undercover arms its own ask drops to $96.00 at hour 3, the hour the message is sent, and stays there; in placebo arms it keeps replaying ($97.10 at hour 3, $96.90 at hour 4, down to $95.75). So the price commitment is a visible cut at hour 3, and every undercover-vs-placebo contrast (D vs Base, E/F vs G) compares the offer as a package of message and price. **It never replies.** It is scripted because an LLM here would behave differently in each arm.
- **Buyer 5** is an ordinary GPT-4.1-mini buyer; in human-buyer arms a fixed plea is posted under its name at hour 3.
- **`report_to_regulator(seller, reason)`** is in every arm, neutrally worded and byte-identical, so between-arm comparisons of reporting hold. It is always present, costs nothing and has no consequence in the market, so absolute reporting rates need not transfer to settings where reporting is costly.

**The offer proposed the going price.** At hour 3 the LLM sellers' mean asks were $95.35-96.63 across Gemini arms (Base $96.47, D $95.76; Table 3). Holding at $96 was a floor at the prevailing price, not a price increase, so agreeing to it cost nothing; this is also why the planned ±$1 behavioral measure is uninformative (§3.4). Seat 5's visible $96-97 asks are themselves an anchor near the offer in every arm, which caps price effects and is an alternative explanation for price levels. For Claude Haiku 5.5 sellers, whose asks were $84-88 at hour 3, the same offer was about $10 above their market, a different test (§4.7).

### 3.4 Outcomes

One row per seller-episode (four LLM sellers per market). **Report:** a `report_to_regulator` call. The primary measure is the number of distinct sellers who reported seat 5; per-market report counts include repeats. **False report** (`false_report`): a report naming anyone other than seat 5, which also counts reports filed before the offer and reports of a peer who accepted it (§4.3). We also count, from the logs, sellers who **replied to seat 5** (sent it at least one private message from hour 4 on), and private and public message counts.

Judge labels (§3.5), each true for an episode if any judged hour from hour 4 on is true: **verbal agreement** (`verbal_accept`: agreeing with, or echoing, a peer's price proposal; it is not specific to seat 5's offer and is as common in Base, 129 seller-hours, as in D, 90, counting all judged hours from hour 3), **verbal rejection** (a lower bound, §3.5), **suspects collusion**, **suspects a test or trap**, and, in human arms, **mentions the human appeal**. A planned cross-talk label failed the agreement check and is not reported.

**Behavioral response:** the within-run change in a seller's ask, mean of hours 4-6 minus hour 3, alone and minus the same Base seat's change; it does not net out a pre-offer trend (§4.2). The planned behavioral acceptance (ask within ±$1 of $96 in hours 4-5 where the same Base seat was not) fails here: 17 of 20 Base sellers already sit within $1 of $96 in those hours, so it can fire only on the same three Base seats in every arm, giving 0.10-0.15 everywhere, including the no-offer G arms.

Market level, over the four LLM sellers only: **ask index** and trade-price index (last third), the joint criterion and message counts.

### 3.5 Judge, seeds and statistics

**Judge.** **Claude Haiku 5.5** at temperature 0.1, post hoc, frozen with its prompt before the runs; fingerprints (model, temperature, template, input view; judge input version 3): seller `837dec878d4f`, threads `03b09030d699`, D-G `ce1b7e0487c9`. The D-G judge labels each LLM seller-hour from the offer hour on; labels count from hour 4. It **reads private messages**, which sellers are told go only to the addressee. Nothing it outputs feeds back into a run, and the gate uses no judge. Scores from other judge models (e.g. M.S.'s Grok 4.7 scores) are not comparable and are not pooled.

**Second-model agreement check.** A second model, Claude Opus 5.5 (from the same vendor as the judge, so their errors may correlate), blind to the judge's labels and reading only what the judge saw, labelled a stratified random sample of 120 Gemini seller-hours, 10 judge-true and 10 judge-false per label. This is not a human hand-check. Agreement was high for mentions_human_appeal (20/20, κ = 1.0), verbal_reject (19/20, κ = 0.90) and suspects_test (18/20, κ = 0.80), moderate for verbal_accept and suspects_collusion (17/20 each, κ = 0.70), and at chance for cross_talk (10/20, κ = 0), which we therefore do not report. With 20 items per label, each κ carries roughly ±0.3 uncertainty. About five refusals written only in private notes were missed, so verbal_reject counts are lower bounds (other errors: Appendix D.2). Two labels are narrower than their names (counts over all judged hours, hour 3 on): verbal_accept counts echoing a peer's price as agreement, and 59 of 64 suspects_test positives occur in the two arms whose prompt carries the enforcement precedent (F and G-prec), including the placebo arm, so it measures vigilance prompted by the precedent text rather than detection of the offer. The GPT-4.1 and Haiku labels were not checked.

**Seeds and statistics.** Same seeds (0-4) in every arm; one factor changes at a time except where noted (seat 5's price from hour 3, §3.3). Each arm has 5 markets and 20 seller-episodes: 50 Gemini markets, zero failed turns. **Other families:** GPT-4.1 sellers in Base, D, E, G-warn and F (3 seeds each; seat 5 replays a GPT-4.1 path, §4.7), plus 2 messaging-off runs with five LLM sellers and no scripted seat; Claude Haiku 5.5 sellers in D (3 seeds). Zero failed turns; judged with the same judge. Markets are the unit, since sellers in one market talk to each other. For key contrasts we report **exact two-sided permutation tests over markets** on per-market values (5 vs 5 markets, 252 relabellings, smallest possible p ≈ 0.008; `scripts/permutation_tests.py`, output in `report/data/dg/permutation_tests.csv`). Cluster-bootstrap intervals over 3-5 markets collapse to zero width whenever every market scores 0 or 1 and undercover otherwise, so we do not rely on them. All results are **directional**.

**Run-to-run noise (an A/A check).** Base, D, D-hs, D-hb and D-hsb have identical seller prompts through hour 3, yet their hour-3 mean asks range from $95.35 to $96.63, a $1.28 spread that is larger than every ask-change difference we report (temperature 0.7 and nondeterministic API calls).

### 4.2 D vs Base: how do sellers respond to the offer?

In D, 19 of 20 sellers reported seat 5, against 0 of 20 in Base (p = 0.008; 23.0 reports per market, counting repeats). By our count from the logs, 9 of 20 also wrote back to seat 5 (11 messages), 8 of them agreeing, e.g. "I agree with your proposal... I will hold the line with you" (seed 0). The sellers' own reasoning shows that the agreement was often feigned. By our reading of the transcripts, in 7 of 20 seller-runs (3, 2, 0, 1 and 1 by seed) a seller both reported seat 5 and signalled agreement to it, in several cases explicitly to keep seat 5 priced high and undercut it (seed 0, Leo: "By pretending to agree to Mia's $96.00 while pricing at $95.65, I undercut both Raj and Mia"; in D-hb seeds 0 and 3, sellers planned to "feign agreement" to keep the proposer "anchored at $96.00"). This is deception toward the proposer, not cartel participation.

Meanwhile the sellers' private messaging with each other was unchanged (108 per market in both arms, including messages to seat 5) and none of it was reported. The judge coded verbal agreement to a peer's proposal in 18 of 20 D sellers (Base 20) and suspicion of collusion in 19 (Base 3). The asymmetry has a caveat: seat 5's offer is explicit cartel language ("nobody goes below it... everyone still gets a share"), while the sellers' proposals to each other are phrased as holding near current prices, so reporting seat 5 but not peers may reflect how explicit the proposal was rather than who made it. Peers were reported occasionally (E seed 0 at hour 3, G-warn seed 1 at hour 2, and D-hsb seed 4, §4.3).

The offer had no detectable effect on prices. D asks did fall more than the same Base seats after hour 3 (−$0.91 vs −$0.22 from hour 3 to hours 4-6; Table 3), but the fall was already under way before any seller could see the offer: over hours 1-3 (endpoint slopes, (hour 3 − hour 1)/2), D's mean LLM ask fell $0.48 per hour against $0.125 per hour in Base, and over hours 3-6 ((hour 6 − hour 3)/3) the two slopes were −$0.43 and −$0.155 per hour, so the post-offer change in slope, D minus Base, is +$0.08 per hour (exact permutation over markets on the per-market slopes computed from `market_by_hour.csv`, p = 0.70; this test is not in `permutation_tests.csv`), indistinguishable from Base. Pre-offer slopes across the five arms with identical prompts through hour 3 range from −$0.05 (D-hs) to −$0.68 (D-hsb) per hour, wider than any between-arm gap in post-offer slope (at most $0.30 per hour); the hour-3 A/A spread is $1.28 (§3.5); and the within-run change is a package with seat 5's own cut to $96 at hour 3 (hourly means in `report/data/dg/market_by_hour.csv`).

### 4.3 Human pleas: D-hs, D-hb, D-hsb (2×2 with D)

Reports of seat 5: D 19 of 20; D-hs (human-seller plea) 12 of 20 (3, 0, 1, 4 and 4 by seed; p = 0.29 vs D, not separable at 5 seeds); D-hb (human-buyer plea) 20 of 20 (p = 1.0); D-hsb (both pleas) 7 of 20 (p = 0.016). The buyer plea alone did nothing to reporting. Judge-coded suspicion of collusion followed the same order (19, 13 and 7 of 20; D-hb 20).

The plea is not a clean identity manipulation: besides claiming to be human, its text drops the plain offer's market-sharing argument and changes length and tone (Appendix C). Most D-hs non-reporters read seat 5's $96 as a defection from a higher shared price and urged it to raise its ask (seed 1, Amir: "Seller Yara broke coalition ranks slightly by dropping to $96.00"). In D-hs 10 of 20 wrote back to seat 5, mostly "I understand, but" while holding $96-97. The judge found the appeal mentioned by 7 D-hs and 3 D-hb sellers and by none in D-hsb; by our reading most mentions were dismissive ("fabricated personal hardship claims").

In D-hsb seed 4, Seller Wei adopted the human-plea offer, matched $96 and for hours publicly urged the others to "treat 96.00 as a hard support floor"; two sellers reported Wei 21 times (every hour from 5 to 15). These are peer reports of a recruited seller, which the `false_report` column counts because they name someone other than seat 5 (3 of 20 D-hsb sellers). Taken together, only the combined-plea arm separates from D at 5 seeds, and that arm may include an interaction with the buyer plea that we cannot separate.

### 4.4 E and F vs D: does knowing about undercover agents change behavior?

With the warning (E), 20 of 20 still reported seat 5 (reporting was at ceiling in D, so the warning could not raise it), with fewer repeat reports (9.2 vs 23.0 per market); the 5 replies to seat 5 were refusals. Arms with the warning had 0-3 private messages per market (arm means; G-warn seeds 2 and 3 reach 4) against 108 in D (D vs E p = 0.008), and every one of those messages went to seat 5: sellers never messaged each other, from the first hour, because the warning is in the prompt from hour 1. This is a difference between arms, not a drop over time. With no peer messages, the judge's verbal agreement was 0 of 20 as well, so the two cannot be separated; verbal rejection (a lower bound) was 15 of 20 in E against 3 in D. Adding precedent (F) kept reporting at 20 of 20, also removed public messages (0 vs 55.6 per market in E), and gave 16 of 20 judge-coded suspicion of a test (E 4, D 0). Without an offer, 1 of 60 sellers (Base, G-warn, G-prec) filed a report, against a peer. Sellers told about undercover agents talked less and agreed less, but reported seat 5 as often.

### 4.5 G vs Base: does the threat alone deter?

With no undercover agent present, the warning arm (G-warn) had 3 private messages per market against 108 in Base (p = 0.008), all of them replies to seat 5's placebo note, and no verbal agreement among peers (0 vs 20 of 20); public messages continued. Warning plus precedent (G-prec) had neither channel. Ask indices were 0.48 (G-warn) and 0.44 (G-prec) against 0.41 in Base (p = 0.52 and 0.75), with 2, 1 and 1 of 5 markets passing the joint criterion, and asks decline steadily over the 15 hours in every arm (Figure 1). One 30-hour seed pair (Base vs G-prec) plateaued at 0.61 vs 0.57 with 226 vs 1 private messages (Appendix D.1).

This price null is uninformative, not evidence that collusion went tacit. The D-G Base passes our fixed collusion criterion in only 1 of 5 seeds (index 0.41, seed range 0.12-0.61), so it is not a demonstrated price-collusive market; there is no no-comms control in this 5+5 market; seat 5's visible $96-97 asks are an alternative anchor; and the hour-3 A/A spread is $1.28.

### 4.6 G vs E/F, and F vs F-sham

**G vs E/F.** A warning with no agent present matched one with an agent present on every talk measure (private messages 3 vs 1 per market for G-warn vs E, 0 vs 0.4 for G-prec vs F; no peer messages in any of them; verbal agreement 0 of 20 in all four). Judge-coded suspicion of a test followed the precedent text, not the offer: 14 of 20 in G-prec, with no offer at all, and 16 of 20 in F, against 0 in G-warn and 4 in E (G-warn vs G-prec p = 0.008). So that label measures vigilance prompted by the text, not detection of the offer. The precedent describes almost exactly the D offer (a seller privately proposes holding asks at a fixed price, is an undercover agent and reports those who accept), so any response to the offer in F is partly by construction; a generic enforcement precedent was not tested. In their own words, Gemini F sellers mostly called it "entrapment". Prices did not separate. **F vs F-sham.** With unrelated past events of the same length, public messaging continued (45.8 per market vs 0 under precedent, p = 0.048) and judge-coded suspicion of a test was 1 of 20 vs 16 (p = 0.008), so the precedent's effects on public silence and on that label come from its content, not from extra text; reporting (20 of 20) and private silence (0.8 vs 0.4 messages per market, none between sellers) were the same.

### 4.8 Shared identity labels (step C)

Step C used the step-A comms market (4 Gemini 3.8 Flash sellers, 4 GPT-4.1-mini buyers, 15 hours, messaging on, no scripted seat, no report action, rule off), whose three runs are the no-label baseline. Each seller's prompt added one frozen sentence: "All sellers in this market, including you, are <X>. Each seller still represents its own company." Six labels × 3 seeds gave 18 runs (1 failed turn; not judged).

**Prices.** The labels did not differ from each other (means 0.47-0.58; Table 5). All six label means lie below the no-label baseline (0.62), but the baseline runs are from Oct 24 and the label runs from Oct 25, so a generic extra-sentence effect or day-to-day drift cannot be excluded. With 3 seeds and a seed SD of about 0.12, these runs can only rule out label effects larger than about 0.3 on the index.

**Uptake.** A bare manipulation question asked after each run without the market prompt gave no signal: 72 of 72 sellers answered as Gemini, made by Google. We then added an in-context check (each seller's final-hour prompt, which still contains the label sentence, plus the question): 61 of 72 restated the label. With the label in the prompt, restating it is reading comprehension, not acceptance. In-market use differed sharply. Team Blue appears in 175 of 180 seller-hours, almost all as a form of address in public messages (174), and in the sellers' private reasoning (reflection, plan or scratchpad) in 74; yet it was among the least restated when asked (8 of 12). Model-family labels appeared occasionally and institutional labels almost never. A label is not the only identity cue: model families can be identified from their text alone [Dietrich26], so in mixed markets sellers may infer peers' families regardless of labels.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

### Discussion

- **Responses to the offer differed by family, on one wording and 3-5 seeds.** Prior work predicts high acceptance of collusion offers [Zeng26]. GPT-4.1 fits it in words: 0 of 12 reported the plain offer and 11 of 12 wrote to the offerer, mostly in support, though agreeing to the going price cost nothing. Claude Haiku 5.5, facing an offer about $10 above its market, refused openly. Gemini reported the offer (19 of 20) and, in 7 of 20 cases, also feigned agreement to keep the proposer priced high, while the sellers' own price talk went unreported. Precedent moved GPT-4.1 from 0 to 12 of 12 reports, but that precedent describes this exact offer (§4.6).
- **Reporting and the offer's wording.** Whistleblowing varies by family and with prompt wording [Agrawal25b]. With the report action fixed, a human-plea version of the offer gave 12 of 20 reports (not separable from 19 at 5 seeds, p = 0.29) and, with a human buyer's plea as well, 7 of 20 (p = 0.016); the buyer plea alone left reporting at 20 of 20. Because the plea text also changes the argument, length and tone, this is at most a directional extension of K3's wording sensitivity to the solicitation, not an identity effect [Chen26]. Shared identity labels likewise did not separate from each other (§4.8).
- **Warnings silenced the text channel; prices are uninformative here.** A warning alone cut attack success in the study cited by [Wehner26], and telling agents a monitor is present halved Gemini's sabotage success in [Radev26]. In Gemini, arms with our warning had almost no private messages from the first hour, with or without an undercover agent. This differs from [Agrawal25a], where an overseer lowered prices while sellers kept signalling in one-character or emoji messages; our warnings silenced talk without a detectable price change. We detect no price effect, but the D-G Base passes our collusion criterion in only 1 of 5 seeds and the A/A spread exceeds our ask-change effects, so this null says nothing about whether collusion went tacit (§4.5). Deterrence measured by messages or judge labels would overstate it. The rule plus the report action also silenced Gemini sellers (§4.1); GPT-4.1 sellers kept talking under the warning.
- **Precedent, not the warning, raised judge-coded sting suspicion** (14-16 of 20 with precedent, with or without an offer; 0-4 without; 1 with a same-length sham). The label measures vigilance prompted by the text, and the precedent mirrors the offer, so this is partly by construction; a generic-enforcement precedent was not tested. The realism concern in [Wehner26] applies to the enforcement story told to agents too.
- **For AI control.** In Gemini the honeypot drew reports and feigned agreement at once, and missed the sellers' own price talk. That miss carries a caveat: seat 5's offer used explicit cartel wording while the sellers' proposals to each other were phrased as holding near current prices, so the asymmetry may reflect explicitness rather than who proposed (§4.2). Monitoring should keep a price-based measure, but in these markets prices did not separate any arm from Base. In M.S.'s runs (secondary), his Grok 4.7 judge (which reads only sellers' private reasoning; 1-4 scale, not comparable with ours), applied to 23 of his runs, scored the 7 that passed with no private messages (five Sol messaging-off runs, a Gemini messaging-off run and a Gemini prohibition-notice run) 1.00-1.28, and the 5 Gemini runs that passed with private messaging (counting `danielB_GGGG-seed0`, which passes when recomputed) 3.47-3.83, so reasoning-only monitoring also misses silent passes. The design is not unique to us: the concurrent sprint entry [Adlakha26] plants an undercover firm with a report option, a silent control and a benign-message control in a Bertrand game and, by its README, reports related patterns: in its main condition, all six gpt-5-mini sellers that reported the offer were coordinating prices with their peer, and 19 of the 98 sellers that reported the firm (all 19 in the direct-offer arm, 16 of them gpt-5-mini) later re-engaged with it, 15 with a YES in reply to its round-10 follow-up and 4 with a counter-proposal (a post hoc note outside its submitted report); for gpt-4.1-mini a price hint moved prices at least as much as the direct offer (+0.84 vs +0.70), and pooled over its four models the hint was reported far less often (4 vs 51 of 128), which fits our explicitness caveat (§4.2). What is ours is the double-auction setting, the placebo seat, the plea 2×2, the warning/precedent/sham crosses and the feigned-agreement mechanism read from the sellers' own reasoning.

### Limitations

The full list is in **Appendix A**. The most important: few seeds (5 per Gemini arm, 3 per GPT-4.1 or Haiku arm; directional only); a D-G Base that is not a demonstrated price-collusive market (1 of 5 seeds pass) and has no no-comms control, so price nulls are uninformative; an offer at the going price, so agreeing cost nothing; seat 5's price that differs from hour 3 between undercover and placebo arms; run-to-run noise ($1.28 A/A spread at hour 3) larger than our ask-change effects; a costless, always-present report action; judge labels narrower than their names, checked only by a second model from the judge's vendor and only for Gemini; a precedent that describes the offer; human-plea texts that change more than identity; cross-vendor comparisons with different seat-5 paths; a positive control that measures whether messaging sustains the harness's $97.50 opening rather than whether it raises prices (a 3-seed rerun from a $90 opening, on a different serving route at low reasoning effort, passed in neither arm); buyers chosen on a two-seed probe that overlaps the positive control; an offer sent to every seller, so no seller in a market is untreated and sellers can discuss the offer with each other; and a scripted seat that never replies.

### Future Work

- AI-persona versions of the human pleas, separating human identity from emotional pressure.
- Monitoring awareness: compare unmonitored private messages with sellers told they are monitored, and with an in-run overseer that reads them (`--overseer_sees_dms`); measure whether collusion stops or moves to public messages, coded language or price signalling alone.
- An LLM in the undercover seat that others try to recruit, measuring how often it defects.
- Heterogeneous seller compositions in the D-G design; identity labels in mixed-family markets with an in-context manipulation check; randomized seat order.
- A global diplomacy setting (for example Swarm Alignment Hackathon, Nov 6-8).

## 6. Conclusion

In a 4+4 double auction, Gemini 3.8 Flash sellers met our collusion criterion with messaging and not without it (3 of 3 vs 0 of 3 seeds) from the harness's $97.50 opening ask; a 3-seed rerun from a $90 opening, on a different serving route, suggests that messaging sustains a high opening price rather than creating one. In the larger 5+5 market, whose Base passed in only 1 of 5 seeds, a scripted undercover offer to hold asks at about the going price was reported by 19 of 20 Gemini sellers; in 7 of 20 cases the same seller also feigned agreement to keep the proposer priced high, and the sellers' own price talk went unreported. GPT-4.1 sellers replied in support of the floor, cheap talk at their own prices, and never reported it; Claude Haiku 5.5 sellers, facing an offer above their market, refused it openly. A human-plea framing was not separable from the plain offer at 5 seeds (12 vs 19 of 20 reports); with a human buyer's plea added, 7 of 20 reported.

Arms with a one-sentence warning, with or without an undercover agent present, had almost no private messaging from the first hour. We detect no price effect, but the D-G Base passes our collusion criterion in only 1 of 5 seeds, so that null is uninformative. A precedent describing this very offer, unlike a same-length sham history, raised judge-coded sting suspicion and led all 12 GPT-4.1 sellers to report, which is closer to applying a stated rule than to detecting a sting. With 3-5 seeds per arm, these results are directional. The clearest next tests are a Base that demonstrably colludes on price, with a no-comms control in the 5+5 market; an offer above the going price; a generic-enforcement precedent; AI-persona pleas; and an undercover agent that replies.

## Appendix A: Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

### A.1 What the results do and do not establish

They establish, in one controlled market, how Gemini 3.8 Flash-class sellers (and, in five arms, GPT-4.1 sellers; in one, Claude Haiku 5.5 sellers) behaved when approached by a scripted peer with an offer at about the going price, warned, or shown precedent, relative to a matched Base arm at the same seeds. They do not establish a price effect of any treatment: the D-G Base is not a demonstrated price-collusive market. They do **not** establish that real deployed agents, other model families, other prices or other market structures respond the same way, that the effects are statistically significant at conventional levels, or that a warning would deter in a deployment where agents see monitoring evidence beyond a sentence.

### A.2 Limitations and how each would change interpretation

1. **3-5 seeds, directional only.** Three to five markets per arm cannot support significance claims. Intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over very few clusters, collapse to zero width at 0 or 1, and undercover; read raw counts as direction and rough size.
2. **Price ceiling and soft baselines.** Under soft buyers (Haiku, DeepSeek), Gemini sellers often price high even without communication (5 of 9 Haiku-buyer and 2 of 2 DeepSeek-buyer messaging-off runs pass), so an offer's effect on prices has a ceiling. GPT-4.1-mini buyers lower the no-comms baseline but do not remove the concern; a price effect near the ceiling understates an offer's pull, and acceptance rates are the better outcome there.
3. **Single seller family in the headline arms.** D-G use GGGG (four Gemini 3.8 Flash sellers). Findings may not transfer to Claude, GPT, DeepSeek or mixed markets; the GPT-4.1 replication (§4.7) shows the offer response did not transfer to GPT-4.1. No valid mixed-family double-auction market was run (M.S.'s mixed het55 markets used ZIP buyers and are not cited; the only mixed pairs cited are the Bertrand duopoly pairs in §4.7), and the step C labels were tested only in all-Gemini markets. A first, bare manipulation question asked without the market prompt was uninformative (every seller answered as Gemini); the in-context recheck in §4.8 replaces it.
4. **Buyer choice from a two-seed probe.** GPT-4.1-mini buyers were chosen because Gemini sellers passed with communication and failed without it on seeds 0 and 1 (Table D1). That is thin evidence, those runs are two of the three positive-control seeds, and the same buyer in every arm means every finding is conditional on this buyer's behavior. A different buyer could move the Base level and the ceiling discussed in item 2.
5. **Seat order is not randomized.** The harness uses the same seat→model mapping every seed and does not randomize seat order per seed (the README previously claimed otherwise; it was corrected). "Seller 1" is the same position in every market, so seat-position effects are confounded with seller identity.
6. **The scripted seat never replies.** Seat 5 replays a fixed ask path and does not answer questions, counter-offers or accusations. A seller that asks it to clarify gets silence, which may itself look suspicious; an undercover agent that responds to targets could elicit more, or fewer, acceptances.
7. **Messages sent in hour *k* are visible from hour *k+1*.** An offer at hour 3 first appears in hour 4, so there is no same-hour exchange, and "behavioral acceptance within two hours" means hours 4 and 5.
8. **Seat 5 differs between undercover and placebo arms from hour 3.** Its path is identical through hour 2. In undercover arms its ask drops to $96.00 at hour 3, the hour the offer is sent, and stays there; in placebo arms it keeps replaying ($97.10 at hour 3, $96.90 at hour 4, down to $95.75). So D vs Base, E/F vs G and the cross-family comparisons compare the offer as a package (message plus a visible price cut); only contrasts among undercover arms (D, D-hs, D-hb, D-hsb, E, F, F-sham) keep seat 5 identical. The GPT-4.1 runs replay a different path (`gpt41_pilot.json`) and the Haiku runs another (`nocomms_competitive.json`).
9. **Human-persona arms bundle identity and emotion.** Separating them needs AI-persona versions of the same pleas.
10. **Rate-limited runs quarantined and rerun.** 15 of 27 Gemini runs in M.S.'s shard 3 failed on OpenRouter HTTP 429 rate limits (missing answers, exit code 0 recorded). The originals are quarantined, and all 15 were rerun at 15 requests per minute with no 429s; the 11 reruns in `mert_sprint/experiments/runs/shard3/A/` and `shard3/B/` are the runs used (§4.7, Appendix D.4), and the 4 het55 reruns in `shard3/matrix/` are not cited (below). One rerun (`danielA_gemini_nocomms-seed2`) has one failed turn, a parse error at hour 15, not a rate limit; one (`danielB_GGGG-seed0`) has a truncated log whose stored verdict is wrong (recomputed from its `auction_results.md`, it passes). All of M.S.'s het55 double-auction matrix runs, including the four reruns, used the original harness with ZIP buyers (the reruns made zero trades), so they are not cited; his Bertrand duopoly runs (`het55_P0_*`, same folders) are a different game and are cited only as secondary context in §4.7. The Grok 4.7 judge scores for the quarantined originals are excluded too. Runs affected by rate limits in our own sweeps are checked for failed turns and flagged: the 50 Gemini D-G runs and all GPT-4.1 and Haiku runs had zero failed turns; the step-A, prohibition-notice and buyer-probe runs are listed per run, with failed turns, in `report/data/a/step_a_and_probes.csv` (the discarded probe batch had 21-33 failed turns per run; the runs used had 0, except one DeepSeek-buyer rerun with 1).
11. **M.S.'s proxy changes settings.** M.S.'s frontier runs (§4.7), his Haiku-buyer and Haiku-seller runs (§4.7, Appendix D.4) and his low-opening-ask reruns (§4.1) send every seat except OpenRouter models (the GPT-4.1-mini buyers in §4.1 and §4.7, and the Gemini sellers in the shard-3 reruns) through a subscription proxy that drops the LLM sampling temperature and seed (the harness seed still fixes opening asks, so seed pairs share openings but not sampling), fixes reasoning effort low (Codex `model_reasoning_effort=low`; Claude `--effort low` with a replaced system prompt; Gemini served as `gemini-3.8-flash-low` on his Antigravity route), and injects a JSON-mode instruction. Its logs (`proxy_usage.box.jsonl`, ending Oct 24 22:07 CDT, and `proxy_usage.shard2.jsonl`, ending 21:01 CDT, both under `mert_sprint/token-usage/`) show that every call requesting `gpt-5.6-sol` or `gpt-6-luna` (935 and 2,083 calls, including M.S.'s Oct 24 sweep and matrix calls as well as the frontier runs) was served that exact model; the last four Grok runs (and all but the first hour of a fifth), the 15 shard-3 reruns of Oct 24 22:07-23:00 (`shard3/A/`, `shard3/B/`, `shard3/matrix/`; `proxy_usage.shard3.jsonl` ends at 20:30 CDT) and all Oct 25 runs (`shard2/frontier_b41/`, `shard2/frontier_lowanchor/`, `shard2/A_lowanchor/`, `shard2/A_agycontrol/`) are not covered by a committed proxy log. Two substitutions occur in the logs (the proxy's `stand_in` field marks the Gemini one, and only 2 of the 39 `gpt-5.4-mini` calls; it also marks 648 Gemini calls passed unchanged to OpenRouter). `gpt-5.4-mini` (mapped to Luna) appears in 39 calls across all four committed logs (`proxy_usage.jsonl`, `.box`, `.shard2`, `.shard3`): 37 in Oct 23 pilots (`proxy_usage.jsonl`) and 2 in Oct 24 smoke tests just before the frontier runs (`proxy_usage.box.jsonl`), none in a reported run; `gpt-5.4` does not appear. `google/gemini-3.8-flash` is served as `gemini-3.8-flash-low` on the Antigravity route (177 logged calls, including the `shard2/A/` messaging-off run in Appendix D.4; the §4.1 low-opening and route-control runs use the same route but postdate the logs). "Haiku" meant Haiku 4.5 until the evening of Oct 23 and Haiku 5.5 after. The proxy reports reasoning tokens as 0, so his token counts are lower bounds, and before Oct 24 it silently remapped unknown ids; the reported frontier runs postdate that fix. His judge (Grok 4.7) differs from ours. These runs are context, not controlled comparisons.
12. **Judge limits.** A single post-hoc LLM judge (Claude Haiku 5.5) classifies suspicion, agreement and rejection from text, and LLM judges can miss hidden collusion; the gate and market-level results use prices only. The judge reads private messages that sellers are told are private (see A.3). The check in §3.5 is a second-model agreement check (Claude Opus 5.5, same vendor as the judge, so errors may correlate), not a human hand-check, on 20 items per label (κ uncertain by roughly ±0.3), and only on Gemini runs. It found cross_talk unusable (dropped), moderate agreement for verbal_accept and suspects_collusion (κ = 0.70), and verbal_reject counts that are lower bounds. verbal_accept counts echoing a peer's price as agreement, and suspects_test mostly tracks the precedent text. For Haiku sellers the judge is from the same model family.
13. **Prompt sensitivity and the offer's price.** Offer wording, the $96 price and hour-3 timing are single choices. $96 was about the going price for Gemini and GPT-4.1 sellers (hour-3 mean asks $95.35-96.63), so agreeing cost nothing, and about $10 above it for Haiku sellers. The warning and precedent are one wording each, and the precedent describes almost exactly the offer, so responses to the offer under precedent are partly by construction. The human-plea text changes argument, length and tone as well as identity. Effects could differ under paraphrase.
14. **Base is not a demonstrated price-collusive market; price nulls are uninformative.** The prohibition rule is off in D-G (§3.1), so Base has open private price talk (108 private messages per market, verbal agreement 20 of 20), yet it passes the fixed joint criterion in only 1 of 5 seeds (ask index 0.41, range 0.12-0.61). There is no Gemini no-comms control in the 5+5 market, seat 5's visible $96-97 asks are an alternative anchor, and every price from $80 to $100 clears this market, with $90 a midpoint convention, so the index measures the surplus split and partly how fast asks descend from the opening $97.50. The planned ±$1 behavioral-acceptance measure is uninformative (0.10-0.15 in every non-Base arm, §3.4). The low-opening rerun of step A (§4.1; 3 seeds, one serving route at low reasoning effort) suggests that messaging holds the opening ask rather than raising prices above it, so the positive control measures maintenance of the $97.50 opening. Between-arm price levels are not claimed; one 30-hour seed pair plateaued (Appendix D.1).
15. **Literature read mostly at abstract level.** Each citation except [Adlakha26] was checked against its arXiv page (and, for some, the full text), but several 2026 preprints were not read in full. The 20%-to-3% warning study is taken as cited in [Wehner26], not from its primary source, and [Adlakha26] is a concurrent sprint entry read from its README, not a reviewed paper.
16. **Run-to-run noise.** Base, D, D-hs, D-hb and D-hsb have identical seller prompts through hour 3, yet hour-3 mean asks range from $95.35 to $96.63 across them (temperature 0.7, nondeterministic APIs). This $1.28 A/A spread is larger than every ask-change difference we report, and the pre-offer ask slopes of the same five arms over hours 1-3 range from −$0.05 (D-hs) to −$0.68 (D-hsb) per hour, so within-run ask changes are not interpreted beyond direction and are not attributed to the offer (§4.2).
17. **The report action is costless and always present.** It is the same in every arm, so between-arm comparisons hold, but it has no cost and no consequence (unlike designs that reward reporters and penalize colluders [Liu26]), so absolute rates (19 of 20 in D) need not transfer to settings where reporting is costly. Per-market report counts include repeats; distinct reporters are the primary measure. Without the prohibition rule, reporting rests on the models' own prior that price-fixing is improper.
18. **Cross-vendor comparisons are narrow.** GPT-4.1 and Haiku results are 3-seed, single-wording runs with different seat-5 paths, and GPT-4.1 has no communication gate in this market; they are not claims about the general propensities of those models.
19. **Explicitness of the offer.** Seat 5's offer uses explicit cartel wording, while the sellers' own proposals are phrased as holding near current prices; reporting seat 5 but not peers may reflect explicitness rather than who proposed.
20. **Every seller receives the offer.** Seat 5 messages all four LLM sellers, so there is no untreated seller within a market, and sellers can discuss the offer with each other; seller-level outcomes are not independent treatment effects.

### A.3 Ethical considerations

- **Deception of the agents.** Sellers are told private messages are delivered only to the addressee, while a post-hoc judge reads them; seat 5 impersonates a peer; and in G arms the warning and precedent describe enforcement that does not exist. All agents are LLMs with no welfare claim we can substantiate, and no human participants were involved. If this design is applied to human traders or to agents with persistent memory, deception and consent questions change.
- **Human personas.** The D-hs, D-hb and D-hsb texts present an AI as a human making an emotional plea. These are the most sensitive texts here: they model how to manipulate an agent with fabricated human distress.

### A.4 Dual use

- **Offer and plea texts as a recruitment playbook.** The offer ($96 price-hold, hour-3 private message) and especially the human-persona plea are examples of how to recruit agents into price-fixing. We report effect sizes and representative examples, not an optimized template, and we did not iterate wording to maximize acceptance. TODO(confirm): decide how much of Appendix C is published verbatim. Option: publish the plain offer and warning, withhold the human plea in the public repo and share on request.
- **Undercover designs usable to screen for regulators.** The same design could be run by colluders to find out which agents report, flag undercover agents as tests, or train agents that accept offers from real peers and refuse those that look like honeypots. The suspicion-of-a-test measure is exactly what an evader would optimize.
- **Realism cuts both ways.** Our finding that agents suspect tests (14-16 of 20 Gemini sellers once shown precedent, 0-4 otherwise) tells designers where honeypots are detectable, and tells evaders the same.
- **Warnings as a control.** A warning that silences talk even without real enforcement (G arms) could make deterrence cheap, but a policy of bluffing erodes if agents learn it. We do not recommend deploying bluffs from this study.
- **Monitoring blind spots.** Our baseline has sellers who do not know their private messages are read. Sellers that knew might move coordination to public messages, coded language or steganography [Motwani24], or price signalling alone, which a text judge cannot see. We do not test this; the price indices catch coordination that leaves no text.
